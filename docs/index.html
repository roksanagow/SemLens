See how a word is used across corpora, one sentence at a time

SemLens is a free tool for exploring, measuring and annotating how a word's meaning differs between collections of text, such as time periods, genres or communities. No programming needed, and it runs on your own computer.

Get started See what it does

What it is for

A language model reads every sentence that contains your word and places similar uses close together. SemLens lets you look at that picture, measure it, and decide for yourself what the senses are.

  • Read, don't trustEvery point, score and table leads back to the sentences behind it.
  • One to ten corporaRaw text files, pasted sentences, or a table of usages with your own columns.
  • Your data stays with youThe model runs on your computer. Nothing you load is uploaded anywhere.
  • Many languagesThe default model is multilingual, and other models can be chosen.

Get started

Three steps, typed into a terminal (Terminal on macOS and Linux, PowerShell on Windows). You need about 4 GB of free disk space and an internet connection for the downloads.

  1. Install uv about 1 minute

    uv is a small program that fetches Python tools and everything they need. You only do this once.

    macOS and Linux
    curl -LsSf https://astral.sh/uv/install.sh | sh
    Windows (PowerShell)
    powershell -ExecutionPolicy ByPass -c "irm https://astral.sh/uv/install.ps1 | iex"

    Then close the terminal and open a new one, so that it knows about uv.

  2. Start SemLens first time: roughly 5–15 minutes

    uvx --python 3.12 --from git+https://github.com/roksanagow/SemLens semlens

    The first time, this downloads SemLens and the software it relies on (a little over 1 GB, mostly PyTorch). How long that takes depends on your connection; the terminal shows progress and it is normal for it to sit on one line for a while. When it is ready, SemLens opens in your web browser.

    Every later time, the same command starts in a few seconds. Keep the terminal window open while you work; closing it stops SemLens.

  3. Try the built-in example first time: a 2 GB download, then a minute or two

    Under Data source, choose Example: “bubble” in the Guardian, 1999–2026, click Load the example, then Embed loaded usages.

    The first time you embed anything, the language model itself is downloaded (about 2.2 GB, once). After that, the 350 example sentences usually take a minute or two on a recent laptop. Then follow the walkthrough below with the real thing in front of you.

Where are my files kept?

Saved corpora, embeddings and analyses go to a folder called .semlens in your home folder; the terminal prints the exact location when SemLens starts. To use another folder, set SEMLENS_HOME before the command, for example SEMLENS_HOME=~/Documents/SemLens uvx …. You can also download any analysis as a single file from inside the app and send it to a colleague.

Does anything leave my computer?

Your texts and annotations do not. SemLens uses the internet for three things only: the downloads above, fetching dictionary definitions from Wiktionary if you ask for them, and downloading another model if you choose one.

Updating, a different port, and installing from the repository
  • To get the newest version, add --refresh right after uvx.
  • Anything after semlens goes to the app server, for example semlens --server.port 8502.
  • To install from a copy of the repository instead, see the README.

A walkthrough with one word

The example is the word bubble in Guardian and Observer articles: 50 sentences from each of seven periods, drawn at random and not chosen for any sense. In 1999 a bubble was mostly dot-com. In 2020 people were told to form one.

1 · Load

A tool for exploring and annotating corpora

SemLens has four workspaces: load your data, explore it, compare it with definitions, and annotate senses. The first screen explains each one.

The example data is one click away under Data source.

Various data formats

Upload plain text files (one per corpus), paste sentences, or upload a table of usages. Extra columns in a table, such as date or source, are kept and come back in your annotation export.

Work can be saved and reopened, as embedded corpora or as a whole analysis.

Select and check sentences

Before anything is computed, read what was found. The concordance shows every occurrence with the word in the middle. Search it, and sort by the word before or after.

2 · Explore

Hundreds of sentences in one overview

Each point is one sentence, coloured by corpus. Sentences the model treats as similar sit close together. Hover over a point to read it.

Three regions are visible before we know what they are.

Where is the tech bubble?

Type words and every sentence containing them is ringed. AI, dot-com, internet and tech give 27 sentences, 13 of them from 1999–2003, and nearly all in the same arm of the plot.

The model was not told these are financial; it placed them together from context.

Compare two corpora by hiding the rest

Click a corpus in the legend to hide it. With only 1999–2003 and 2020–2021 showing, the ringed sentences mention support, travel, Covid or household: none in the first period, nine in the second.

The choice is remembered when you change the view.

Other views of the same points

PCA, UMAP, t-SNE and LDA are different ways of turning the model's high-dimensional vectors into a flat picture. Each view's settings are in Plot options.

The coordinates of any view download as a spreadsheet file.

3 · Measure

Comparing corpora against each other: APD and PRT

Scores turn two clouds of points into one number. APD is the average distance over every cross-corpus pair of sentences. PRT is the distance between the two corpus averages.

The formula sits under each heatmap. On PRT, 2020–2021 stands furthest from the earliest period.

Every corpus against every other: AMD and SAMD

AMD asks, for each sentence, how far its closest counterpart in the other corpus is. SAMD does the same but lets each sentence be matched only once. They react when a sense appears or disappears, even if the average barely moves.

These two come from our paper.

Looking at changes along a timeline

When your corpora are in time order, the same scores are drawn as lines: each period against the one before (solid) and against the first (dashed).

Against 1999–2003, PRT climbs to a peak in 2020–2021, then falls back part of the way.

Read the sentences behind the scores

For any two corpora, see the sentences that are furthest from any counterpart in the other one. These are the candidates for a new or lost sense.

Names and fixed phrases surface here too, which is useful: you can see the noise.

Closest sentences to “AI bubble”

Pick one sentence and see its nearest neighbour in every other corpus. For an AI bubble from 2024–2026, each earlier period offers another speculative bubble, at very small distances: dot.com, property, internet, Bitcoin.

New wording, old sense.

Closest to “support bubble”

The same lookup for a support bubble from 2020–2021 finds nothing close before 2020. The nearest earlier sentences are social bubbles, five to ten times further away.

A new sense, and a hint of where it came from.

4 · Definitions

Definition space

Write the senses you expect, one definition per line. The model reads each one, and every sentence is then described by how far it is from each definition.

The definitions are your hypotheses. Change them and rebuild in seconds.

Or start from a dictionary

Fetch the word's entries from Wiktionary, choose the parts of speech to keep, then edit the list by hand.

Usage labels such as (economics) and (figurative) are kept.

Financial sense against social sense

Now the axes mean something. Horizontal: distance to “asset prices rise beyond a sustainable value”. Vertical: distance to “an insulated social environment”. Stars mark the definitions.

Sentences near the left are financial; sentences near the bottom are social.

How close is each corpus to one definition?

For one definition, see how each corpus is spread around it. For the financial definition, the middle sentence of 1999–2003 sits at a distance of 0.16; by 2024–2026 it is at 0.48.

These are distances, not sense counts. For counts, someone has to decide.

5 · Annotate

Blind annotation, without seeing the corpora

Set your own sense labels. Click a point to label it, or draw a lasso around a group and check it by reading. Blind mode removes the corpus colours and labels, so you cannot label by date.

SemLens never assigns a sense for you.

Visualise annotated sentences

Diamonds show the sense you gave, the dot inside shows the corpus. Where the model's picture and your reading agree, the regions line up; where they do not is where to look.

The labels in this example are an illustration, not a published annotation.

Sense frequencies by corpus

As you annotate, SemLens keeps a table and chart of how often each sense occurs in each corpus. It downloads as a table, and the annotations download with your original columns.

Example labels: the pattern shown is an illustration of the output, not a finding.
